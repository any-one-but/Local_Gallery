Paper & Ink

A printed page rendered as software, with the window chrome of an early desktop drawn on top of it. This is the complete specification — every token, every control, every rule — written so the language can be rebuilt in an application that shares none of its features.

01The sensibility

Warm paper stock, dark brown-black ink, one red used the way a second plate is used in letterpress, and hairline rules doing the work that borders and drop shadows do elsewhere. Nothing glows, nothing blurs, nothing floats on a soft cushion.

Eight principles govern every decision. They are numbered because they are ordered: when two of them conflict, the earlier one wins.

  1. Ink is drawn, not simulated

    Every edge is a hard edge. Borders are solid colour at 1px or 1.5px. Shadows are offset solid blocks with zero blur, the way a shadow is drawn in a woodcut. Gradients exist in exactly one form — a repeating 1px pinstripe — and never as a smooth ramp.

    The reason is legibility at small size. The interface runs at 9–15px; a soft shadow at that scale is mud, and a hard 2px offset is a shape you can read.

  2. One accent, spent carefully

    There is a single accent colour and it is a cost. It marks the primary action, the focused item's spine, the destructive confirmation, and hover on an otherwise quiet control. If a screen carries more than a few accent marks, the accent has stopped meaning anything and the screen is wrong.

  3. Colour is a channel, not a finish

    Nothing is coloured because colour would look nice there. Every use of hue answers a question the user is actually asking. Anything that answers no question is paper, ink, or rule.

  4. Structure is stated by containment

    Grouping is drawn — a real border, a real header — never implied by whitespace or tint alone. The little window is the fundamental unit, and the interface is a desk with several of them on it, even when they are grid areas that cannot be moved.

  5. Density is high and even

    An expert tool, expected to be looked at for hours. Rows run 19–26px, control padding is single-digit pixels, the base face is 13px. Density comes from tightening padding — never from shrinking type below its floor or dropping borders.

  6. Motion is near-static

    Three transitions exist in the whole language, the longest 160ms, and two of them exist only to make a physical mechanism read as physical. Nothing slides, fades in on load, or eases into place.

  7. Never redraw the user's own text

    Where text is marked, the marks are painted behind the real glyphs and the marking layer renders its copy of the text transparent. A metric mismatch can then misplace a rectangle, which is a visible bug; it can never produce a ghosted second copy of the text, which is a disaster. The least negotiable rule here.

  8. The reason lives next to the rule

    The stylesheet is commented in prose, explaining why a value is what it is wherever that is not obvious. A rule with a non-obvious constant and no comment is unfinished. This document extends that habit rather than replacing it.

Deriving a theme

A theme is a complete redefinition of the token block and nothing else. Four rules make a derived theme correct, and the second and fourth are the ones that get missed.

  1. Paper and ink swap roles; the ramps do not invert. In dusk the sheet still sits above the desk. Only the absolute lightness of the whole set moves.
  2. --ink-faint stops tracking the ramp at the dark end. In light the ink ramp descends in contrast; in dusk it ascends. Faint must be the closest of the three to the paper in both, which means it is the darkest on dark stock. Deriving it by inversion produces faint text that shouts.
  3. The accent lightens and warms on dark stock (#C6452A → #E2724F), and so do the supports. --accent-ink follows the paper, not the ink: it is whatever is legible on top of the accent, which is light stock in both themes.
  4. The shadow is always darker than the surface, never --ink. In light, ink is the darkest thing available, so the shadow borrows it. In dusk, ink is pale and a shadow made from it would be a highlight — dusk hard-codes #100E0D. Never write 3px 3px 0 var(--ink) in a theme-agnostic rule; always go through --shadow.

The three-signal system

The signals are disjoint. Reusing a hue for a second meaning is the fastest way to destroy the language.

Accent

“What do I do?”

The primary button. The focused item's spine. The destructive confirm. Hover on a quiet control. A drop line. A warning. Never for something merely on, never for something merely selected.

Teal

“What is this?”

A switch that is on. A run of text that came from elsewhere. An annotation band. A container's own identity. An addition in a diff. Never the primary action, never a selection cursor.

Mustard

“Which one?”

The chosen one of a set: a selected pip, the current row's wash, the highlight tying two panes together. Never on/off, never provenance.

Tints

Where a hue must colour an area rather than a line it is mixed with color-mix(in srgb, …). Reuse a rung rather than inventing a percentage. Tints mix into transparent when they sit over an unknown ground, into a surface token when they replace it, and never exceed 30% — above that the ink on top loses contrast.

The tint ladder
Whisper · 5–7%A surface different in kind — a focused editor's ground
Faint · 10–13%A banded region — an annotation, a header, an even row
Light · 14–17%An inline highlight that must not fight its glyphs

The two permitted hard-coded colours

The dusk shadow #100E0D, and the modal pair — scrim rgba(20,16,14,.4) and dialog shadow 6px 6px 0 rgba(20,16,14,.35). Both are deliberately theme-independent: a scrim derived from a pale dusk ink would lighten the screen instead of darkening it. Everything else goes through a token.

A theming trap

A custom property whose value contains var() is substituted where it is declared. Declaring --ribbon: var(--accent) on :root captures the light accent permanently and no theme will ever update it. Declare such derived properties on the component instead, where they are re-evaluated under whichever theme is in force.

Selection

Text selection inverts rather than tinting — ::selection { background: var(--ink); color: var(--card) }. This matches the way a selected row is drawn: in this language, selected means inverted, for both text and objects.

03Typography

Three system stacks, no webfont. That is a performance position and an aesthetic one: the interface should look assembled out of what the machine already had. The families are a semantic axis, which is the most distinctive thing about the typography here — a screen that uses them consistently is readable without reading.

The caption treatment

One recurring object does every section heading and small structural label: 10px, .14em tracking, uppercase, bold, faint. A pane title is the same idea one notch stronger — 11px, .1em, at full ink.

Tracking is used in exactly four places and each has a fixed value: .14em captions, .1em pane titles, .08em uppercase micro-badges, and ±.01em optical correction on large display type. There are no other values.

Numerics, weight, italics

Any number that can change while it is being looked at carries font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums, so digits do not shift the layout as they tick. Weight is binary — normal or 700, with no intermediate. Italic appears in exactly two roles: the wordmark, and empty-state prose, where it signals the application speaking about an absence rather than content.

04Line & shape

Two border weights, and they mean different things

This binary carries information. 1.5px is a real edge — the boundary of a window, card, button, chip, switch; anything that is an object, and almost always in ink. 1px is a rule — a division inside one object, in --rule or --rule-soft. An object's outline is therefore always ink and always 1.5px, and interior structure is quieter by a full step. A 1px ink border appears only on micro-objects where 1.5px would visually close the shape.

A dashed border is never decorative — it always means not yet or not quite: the add-a-new-one affordance, a comparison target, an item held off. A dotted border means derived: the underline beneath a run of text that came from somewhere else.

Radius

The rule is counter-intuitive and worth stating plainly: the larger the object, the smaller the relative rounding. A pane is 3px on a 400px box, essentially square; a chip is 10px on a 17px box, a full pill. Big surfaces read as sheets of paper, small ones as tokens.

The press

Pressable objects have a physical press, and it is the shadow that moves: the object travels exactly its own shadow offset and the shadow is removed, so it appears pushed flat against the page. The travel must always equal the shadow offset — that is what makes it read as physical rather than as a wobble.

The z-index scale is small, fixed, and every value deliberate — nothing uses an arbitrary large number. 06Surfaces

The desk

The page ground is --paper-deep under a dot grid: a 1px dot every 4px at 4.5% black. Almost subliminal — its job is to make the ground read as a texture behind the sheets rather than as a flat void. It is pure black at low alpha rather than a token, so it darkens both themes identically. This page is sitting on it.

The pane

The little window is the core object. Four details are load-bearing: the 22px header is separated from the body by a 1.5px ink rule — the only interior division drawn at object weight, because a title bar reads as its own object; overflow: hidden clips content to the rounded corner; min-height: 0 and min-width: 0 stop a flex or grid child refusing to shrink below its content; and the body owns the scroll, with bars as flex: none siblings outside it.

The pinstripe

The signature texture, and it appears on title bars only. Two densities encode hierarchy: tighter and darker stripes mean a more important bar. No third density exists.

Two densities
Pane title · rule every 3px
Inner container · rule-soft every 4px

Text never sits directly on the pinstripe. A title is drawn on an opaque patch of --card that interrupts the stripes — a direct quotation of the classic desktop title bar, and mandatory: anything placed in a striped bar must carry its own opaque ground or the stripes will run behind its glyphs and destroy them.

The title-bar button

13px square, empty, ink-outlined, filling with accent on hover. It carries no glyph — position and tooltip say what it does, exactly as a classic window close box did. This is the only deliberately unlabelled control in the language.

The dialog and the toast

A dialog is a pane with a deeper shadow, a 24px header, a body padded 16px 18px, and a sunk foot with buttons pushed right. The primary action is rightmost and is the only accent element on screen. The toast is inverted — ink ground, card text — because it is the application interrupting; bottom-centred, never interactive, gone in about 1.7 seconds.

Toast and keycap
Prompt copied ⌘ ⇧ C — a 1px box with a 2px underside reads as a key with a lip, at 10.5px, with no other effects.

The transparent 1px border is reserved space, so hover cannot shift the row and the keyboard cursor has somewhere to live. Selection and cursor are separate states and can both be visible at once.

Two traps inside a row

The inversion trap. On a selected row the text colour becomes --card. Any descendant that keeps its own pale background — an input, a light-faced button — inherits that pale text and disappears. Such controls must restate their colour explicitly. This has bitten the codebase more than once.

The hover-reveal limit. Hiding a tertiary action until hover is correct for a rarely-used overflow control. It is wrong for a row's primary action: a control reached for constantly must be visible at rest, with a drawn edge and a real hit area. Judge by frequency, not by tidiness.

Creation, filters, badges
×2 held off group

Creation is always a dashed, ghosted, full-width block sitting at the end of the list it adds to — never in a toolbar. Dashed, because it is a placeholder for a thing that does not exist yet. A badge is never filled: it is an outline, so it cannot be mistaken for a chip.

Six requirements, all mandatory

  1. color: transparent on the backdrop. The visible glyphs are always the real control's. A ghosted second copy of the text becomes impossible by construction.
  2. Family, size, line-height, white-space and wrapping match exactly between the layers, or marks drift from words.
  3. Horizontal padding moves from the backdrop to its line elements, so a line's band spans the full width while its text still starts where the control's text starts. Vertical padding stays on the backdrop.
  4. An empty line needs a zero-width space. An empty block collapses to zero height and every mark below slides up a line.
  5. The wrapper scrolls, not the control. A scrollbar inside the control narrows its content box relative to the backdrop's and shifts every mark. Grow the control to its content height, hide its overflow, and let the wrapper scroll — the backdrop then scrolls with it for free and needs no synchronisation.
  6. pointer-events: none on the backdrop, always.

Two-pane linkage

Where the same content appears in two panes, hovering either lights both, in mustard. The container lights with a left border and a faint wash, the individual part with a stronger one. The padding-left: 7px; margin-left: -9px pair lets a 2px indicator appear outside the text's own left edge without moving the text — the standard way this language attaches a spine to a block.

10Glyphs

There is no icon font and there are no SVG icons. Every symbol is a Unicode character rendered in the UI face — so it shares the text's metrics and colour, inherits currentColor for free, and cannot fail to load.

The single exception

A shape that must keep a constant stroke width while its container stretches cannot be built from CSS borders or clip-paths, and is drawn as a fixed-size inline SVG with a real stroke and stroke-linejoin: round. Two offset clip-path polygons mitre to a spike where a notch closes; a stroked path does not. Such a piece is composed from a stretching CSS box for the body and a fixed SVG for the shaped end, overlapping by 1px so no seam shows.

11Layout

Gutters are tracks, not gaps

The application fills the viewport below a fixed menu bar and is a grid of panes. The 10px columns between panes are real grid tracks holding the drag handles, so the visible gutter is the grabbable object. A collapsed pane sets both its own track and its gutter track to zero, so no empty gutter is left behind.

minmax(0, 1fr) on the flexible column is required, not 1fr — the latter has an auto minimum and will refuse to shrink below its content.

Resizing: the clamp never writes back

Sizes are stored as numbers and applied as custom properties. One rule matters more than the mechanics: the stored number is what the user asked for, and the applied number is what currently fits. Clamping the stored value means a briefly narrow window shrinks the panes permanently.

The corollaries: a drag is clamped as it happens, against the other pane's current size, so the edge tracks the pointer without rubber-banding and one pane never shoves its neighbour aside; when the window itself is too small, a defined pane yields first, in a fixed order; and double-clicking a handle restores the default, so no drag can be a trap.

Scroll ownership

  • Exactly one scroll container per pane, and it is the body.
  • Every ancestor of it in a flex or grid chain carries min-height: 0.
  • Toolbars and footers are flex: none siblings of the scroller, never inside it — a toolbar that scrolls away takes its controls out of reach.
  • Wide content scrolls inside its own overflow-x: auto box; the page body never scrolls sideways.
  • A scrolling list's section headers may be sticky at z-index: 2, carrying the surface colour.

One breakpoint

Below 1080px the columns become rows and the gutter tracks disappear with them. There is exactly one breakpoint — the language has a wide arrangement and a narrow one, not a progressive reflow.

This is a pointer-and-keyboard language for a desktop application; a touch adaptation would scale the whole system rather than patching individual controls. Within that, the floor is 13px for any target and 19px for anything used frequently.

Spacing comes from a short set — 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 22 — rather than a strict 4 or 8px grid. Odd values exist because at this density optical centring beats arithmetic: 7px above and 6px below is what makes a 17px chip row look even.

12Words

The visual language extends to the writing, and the two are not separable.

  • Sentence case everywhere except captions and pane titles, which are uppercased by CSS rather than by the copy. Never Title Case A Button.
  • Buttons are verbs — Copy prompt, Add an option, Duplicate. Not Submit, and not a noun alone.
  • Tooltips say what will happen, in words, naming the object: “Write {{Audience}} at the caret”, not “Insert”.
  • Empty states tell you what to do next, italic in faint ink: “No variables yet. Write {{a name}} in the text, then click it below the editor.”
  • Explain a refusal rather than hiding it. A disabled control's tooltip says why and how to change it: “Only one option — open it to add more.”
  • Counts carry a unit — 12 words, ×2, 3 of 47. A bare number appears only where a column heading supplies the unit.
  • Em dashes for asides, and typographic quotes around user-supplied names: Delete “Tone”?

13Recipes & refusals

Adding a component

  1. Decide whether it is an object (1.5px ink border, 2–3px radius, shadow) or a region (1px rule border, sunk ground, no shadow). This one decision settles most of the styling.
  2. Choose the surface: card for content, card-sunk for chrome.
  3. Pick the type — display for user-authored names, mono for material and identifiers, sans for everything else — then a size from the scale.
  4. Take every state from the state model. Do not invent a state treatment.
  5. If it needs a hue, prove it answers do / what / which. If not, it is ink.
  6. If it is pressable, give it the resting shadow and the matching travel.
  7. Add min-width: 0 to any flex child holding ellipsised text.
  8. Comment the non-obvious constants, in prose.

Adding a theme

Redefine the whole token block under a scope, obeying the four derivation rules, and set color-scheme. Change nothing else — if a component needs a per-theme override, that component is reaching around a token and should be fixed instead.

Adding a text mark

Pick from the existing four shapes rather than inventing a look: band for a whole line, tinted-inline for a run, underline-only for a weak signal, strike for excluded. Colour it from the three-signal system, apply it in the backdrop layer. Never mark by changing the text's own colour or weight — that would mean redrawing the user's glyphs.
